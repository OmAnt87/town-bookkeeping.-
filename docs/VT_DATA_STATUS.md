# Vermont data status

Generated by `node scripts/vt/status.mjs`. 147 governments loaded. Vermont's towns and cities are its main local governments. Incorporated villages are a second layer inside a town, so village residents pay both. Counties have small budgets, mostly the courthouse; the state pays most of the cost of sheriffs and state's attorneys. Public schools are run by school districts and paid for through the statewide education tax. Each county's file holds the county and the governments in it.

## Sources
- **Revenue, spending and debt:** what each government reported to the U.S. Census Bureau's [Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/gov-finances.html) (individual unit files), FY 2022-2024, with the same mapping as the Carolinas and Delaware. The Census counts Vermont's towns as township governments. 2022 is a Census of Governments year covering every government; later years cover a sample, so each government's latest reported year is used. Vermont has no statewide compilation of town finances; towns publish annual reports and audits individually.
- **Population:** U.S. Census Bureau 2025 population estimates. **Map location:** Census 2024 Gazetteer (county subdivisions and places).

## How Vermont differs
- **One tax bill, several governments.** Each town and city collects the state education property tax (homestead and non-homestead), which pays for schools through the state Education Fund, and the county tax, and pays them over. 84 of the 140 cities, towns and villages reported the whole levy to the Census as their own property tax. For those, only the town's own share is counted: what its spending needed beyond its other revenue, which is how the town rate is set. That also means they show no surplus or deficit for the year. Each affected town's report says so and gives the amounts.
- **Villages are counted separately from their towns.** A village's report covers the village government only (often an electric or water utility); its residents also pay the town, which has its own report.
- **Payments to other governments are left out**, as in the Carolinas and Delaware.

## Known gaps
- **Political money is not scored.** City, town and village candidates do not file campaign finance reports with the state, and contributions to county candidates were not loaded.
- **Not in the Census files:** unorganized towns and gores (mostly in Essex County) have no government of their own.
- **School and county assessments filed as town spending (left out, 2):** Town of Addison ($785,000), Town of Wolcott ($2,454,000). Each of these towns, with no school spending of its own, reported most of its budget as "other general government", which is almost certainly its assessments; that spending is treated as a payment to other governments, and the town's property tax is cut to its own share. Smaller assessments filed the same way cannot be told apart from real overhead and remain in it.
