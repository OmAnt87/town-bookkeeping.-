## Sources
- **Revenue and spending:** MA Department of Revenue, Division of Local Services (DLS) [Municipal Databank](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/division-of-local-services-municipal-databank): each city and town's Schedule A general-fund revenues and expenditures, as filed after the close of each fiscal year (July 1 to June 30). Actual results, not budgets. History back to FY 2016.
- **Property tax:** DLS certified tax levies by class, for the same fiscal year.
- **Debt:** DLS municipal debt dashboard: total outstanding long-term debt (Schedule A Part 10).
- **Population:** U.S. Census Bureau 2025 population estimates. **Map location:** Census 2024 Gazetteer.
- **Political money:** MA Office of Campaign and Political Finance ([OCPF](https://www.ocpf.us/)) itemized receipts of candidates for mayor, and for city council where council candidates file with the state, since 2023.

## How Massachusetts differs
- **Cities and towns run the schools.** Local schools and each town's share of any regional school district are paid from the general fund, so school spending is on each report. It is left out of the services and overhead shares so towns compare with states where school districts are separate governments.
- **State assessments come out of state aid.** "Intergovernmental assessments" (charter school tuition, school choice, transit and county charges) are counted with schools.
- **Fixed costs are reported on their own.** Health insurance, pensions and other benefits (including for school staff) are spread across departments, schools included, in proportion to their size.
- **Taxes include excises.** Schedule A reports all taxes together. The certified property tax levy is shown as property tax and the rest (motor vehicle excise, local-option meals and room excises, penalties and interest) as other taxes and excises. Collections in a year can differ slightly from the levy, so the split is close but not exact.

## Known gaps
- **Political money covers 47 cities** ($1,372,934 in all): candidates for mayor, and for city council in the cities whose council candidates file with OCPF. Everyone else running for city or town office, including every town's select board, files with the city or town clerk, so most towns are not scored on political money. Counted: contributions from PACs and from unions and associations. Business and corporate contributions to candidates are banned in Massachusetts. Not counted: individuals, party and candidate committees.
- **Red flags:** 145 of 351 governments have at least one scored red flag, from police surveillance programs in the [EFF Atlas of Surveillance](https://atlasofsurveillance.org/) (plate readers, drones, gunshot detection, face recognition and similar; 449 records matched) and license-plate cameras mapped in OpenStreetMap, as shown by [DeFlock](https://deflock.org/) (886 cameras inside a government's borders). Any Flock camera counts; other camera brands count only when the government is the recorded operator. Red flags are only as complete as public reporting, and most records give no dollar amount, so they are scored by count. 329 records for access every police department in the state gets through a statewide contract are shown but not scored.
- **Atlas records not matched** to a government (1), because the name fits more than one (a city and a town of the same name), the government is not loaded, or Atlas has the name or county wrong: Baltimore Police Department (Baltimore County).
- **Transparency:** one of the six practices is checked from state records: whether each government has filed its Schedule A annual financial report with DLS for the latest year most have filed. 5 of 351 have not (Town of Florida, Town of Gosnold, Town of Gill, City of Holyoke, Town of Hudson). The other five practices (budget and checkbook online, competitive bidding, recorded meetings, conflict-of-interest disclosures) have no statewide source yet, so they are left out of the score.
- **Older year shown** because a newer Schedule A is not filed yet: Town of Florida (FY 2024), Town of Gosnold (FY 2024), Town of Gill (FY 2024), City of Holyoke (FY 2023), Town of Hudson (FY 2023).
- Enterprise funds (often water and sewer), regional school districts, fire and water districts and other separate authorities are not included. Other financing sources (such as bond proceeds) and transfers from a town's own funds are left out because they are not new revenue.
- The statewide report does not separate outside legal and engineering costs from general government, so "consultants" is not broken out.
