Most public schools are run by separate school districts, but several cities run their own school departments. Each county's file holds the county and its cities and towns.

## Sources
- **Revenue, spending and debt:** what each government reported to the U.S. Census Bureau's [Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/gov-finances.html) (individual unit files), FY 2022-2024, with the same mapping as the Carolinas and Delaware. The Census counts New Hampshire's towns as township governments. 2022 is a Census of Governments year covering every government; later years cover a sample, so each government's latest reported year is used. The Department of Revenue Administration collects each town's annual financial report (MS-535) but publishes them only as individual documents, and its website refused connections from the build environment.
- **Population:** U.S. Census Bureau 2025 population estimates. **Map location:** Census 2024 Gazetteer (county subdivisions).

## How New Hampshire differs
- **One tax bill, several governments.** Each town and city collects the whole property tax bill, including the shares for the school district, the county and the state education tax, and pays them over. 86 of the 116 cities and towns reported the whole levy to the Census as their own property tax. For those, only the town's own share is counted: what its spending needed beyond its other revenue, which is how the Department of Revenue Administration sets the town rate. That also means they show no surplus or deficit for the year. Each affected town's report says so and gives the amounts.
- **City school departments.** Cities such as Manchester, Nashua, Dover, Rochester and Somersworth run their own schools, so school spending is part of their reports; school spending is left out of the services and overhead shares, as in Connecticut and Massachusetts.
- **Payments to other governments are left out**, as in the Carolinas and Delaware.
